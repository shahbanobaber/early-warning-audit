Early-warning audit

Universities increasingly use platform logs to warn students who are falling behind. This page tests one such warning as a decision rather than a score: when it can still help, who it misses, and whether a missing trace means the same thing for every student.

Data from 32,593 students on seven Open University courses, 2013–2014. The model trains on earlier course runs and is tested on the next one. Every number below comes from that test.

1. A later warning is more accurate, but reaches no more students

Each week the warning ranks the students still enrolled, and tutors contact the top of the list. Drag the week. Accuracy climbs, but the students it would catch keep leaving.

3. The students it misses look like students who pass

The traces pick up disengagement: going quiet, missing deadlines. Students who stay busy and still fail look like everyone else.

4. Equal misses, but the scores flatten real differences

The warning misses about the same share of at-risk students in every group. Its risk scores, though, barely move between groups whose real risk differs a lot. The model never sees group membership.

How much does the absence of a trace raise the chance of failing or withdrawing? For students who declared a disability, every trace is weaker evidence, because their risk is high even when the trace is there.

How it was made

  • Features use only the past. Each week the model sees clicks, recency, regularity, forum and quiz use, and submission timing dated before that point. A test checks that nothing later leaks in.
  • Only reachable students count. Students who have already withdrawn are counted as out of reach, not scored.
  • Train on the past, test on the next run. Gradient boosting, checked against logistic regression and a no-model inactivity rule. Marks count only once a tutor would have them back (14 days after the deadline, an assumption).
  • Repeated on a second year. Trained on 2013 only, tested on February 2014. Switch the test year above.

Data: Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (Kuzilek, Hlosta and Zdrahal, 2017), CC BY 4.0. Framing: Domino and Shaffer (2025), who propose judging traces on validity, reliability and equitability. Methods: equal opportunity (Hardt et al., 2016), calibration and its trade-off with equal error rates (Kleinberg et al., 2017), bootstrap intervals (Efron, 1979), permutation importance (Breiman, 2001). This page shows only aggregate results; no student-level data is included.
